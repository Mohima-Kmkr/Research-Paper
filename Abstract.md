# Women, Beauty, and Patriarchy: A Feminist Reading of Fairness Cream Advertisements

---

## 1. Abstract

This study looks at how ideas about beauty, women, and patriarchy are shown in fairness cream advertisements. Today, advertisements play an important role in shaping what people think is “beautiful.” Many of these ads show fair skin as more attractive and link it with success, confidence, and a better life. This research studies how such messages are created and how they support traditional ideas about women and society.

The study uses ideas from feminist theory, the male gaze, beauty standards, and postcolonial thinking about skin colour. It tries to understand how these advertisements show women, what kind of beauty they promote, and how they repeat common gender roles. It also looks at how audiences understand and react to these messages.

This research uses a qualitative method. It studies 10–15 fairness cream advertisements from TV, YouTube, print, and digital media. The analysis focuses on storylines, before-and-after changes, use of skin colour, body language, promises of success in jobs or marriage, and the message given through voiceovers.

The study expects to show that these advertisements not only reflect society but also help in maintaining unfair beauty standards and gender inequality. It also explores how women viewers may accept, question, or reject these ideas, showing that audiences are not always passive but can think critically about media messages.

## 2. Why I Chose This Topic (Modern Research Perspective)

I chose this topic because beauty standards continue to shape how women see themselves, especially in today’s world of digital media and constant advertising. Even though there is more awareness now about inclusivity and body positivity, fairness cream advertisements are still very common, particularly in South Asian societies. These ads often promote the idea that fair skin is linked to success, confidence, and social acceptance, which made me question how deeply these messages influence people.

As a media student, I have always been interested in how visual content affects the way we think and behave. These advertisements are something we see almost every day, yet we rarely stop to critically examine them. This research gives me the opportunity to look beyond the surface and understand the deeper meanings behind these representations. At the same time, I also want to explore how audiences, especially women, respond to these messages, whether they accept them, question them, or resist them.

Overall, this topic feels both academically relevant and personally meaningful, as it connects media theory with real-life experiences in a rapidly changing media environment.
